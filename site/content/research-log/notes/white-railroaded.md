## Introduction (¶1–40) — CORE THESIS; STANFORD
- ¶6: “The transcontinentals, more obviously than other railroads, were entwined with the state. Governments subsidized them, secured their rights of way, regulated them, and protected them. The first transcontinentals began as hybrid public/private enterprises and ended up as private corporations, but their public roots remained.”
- ¶10: the railroads helped create “the modern corporate lobby.”
- ¶12: “It was not ‘capital’ that built the railroads but credit, and the capital that was ultimately at risk in the railroads did not belong to the men who controlled them.”
- **¶22 (mechanism):** “the transcontinentals’ simultaneous failure as businesses and success as sources of individual fortunes for insiders … corporate containers for financial manipulation and political networking … The financiers made money through subsidies, the sale of securities, insider companies for the construction of the railroads themselves, and land speculation. Each funneled corporate resources into private pockets. To do this, they needed considerable […]”
- ¶24: the network of “friends”: “Corporations used the federal government to punish rival corporations while gaining advantages for themselves. They made politics a realm of private competition.”
- ¶33: names Leland Stanford among the promoters; “They were, however, not that smart … there is a Sorcerer’s Apprentice quality to them.”
- ¶35: written over about 12 years while teaching at the University of Washington and Stanford.
- **¶37 (Cardinal thesis line):** “At Stanford, itself a monument to a railroad fortune, I noticed something else. I came to the Silicon Valley in the midst of the dot.com boom at a time when very many people were becoming very rich by creating companies, or owning the securities of companies, that lost vast amounts of money … Eventually, I came to think of these […]”
- ¶40: antimonopolism “was racist to its core” in the West.

## Ch. 1 “Genesis” (¶1–105) — CORE: Associates, 1864 Act, land grants, insider construction companies
- Epigraph ¶1–2, Newton Booth: “It is easier, more delightful, and more profitable to build with other peoples’ money than our own.” (Source n100: Booth’s Aug. 1, 1874 speech, in Crane ed.)
- ¶3–23 cover Civil War railroading: Tom Scott’s corruption (systematic bribery of the Pennsylvania legislature over the tonnage tax, ¶12), war rates, Fink on fixed costs, standard gauge (Lincoln 1863, ¶23). Background only.
- ¶25–45 cover Jay Cooke and Salmon Chase, the bond-selling model, private favors (¶33–37) and a securities primer (Fisk & Hatch). This is the template for Gilded Age “friendship.” Background.
- ¶48: Senator Henry Wilson: “the liberality of this government, either by money or land.” This led to “hothouse capitalism.”
- ¶50: Theodore Judah, Central Pacific chief engineer, was also clerk of the House subcommittee on the Pacific railway bill and secretary of the Senate Pacific Railroad committee. He “used these positions to distribute Central Pacific stock, with a par or face value of $66,000, where he thought it could do the most good.” [n43]
- **¶51:** “a collection of Sacramento storekeepers who called themselves the Associates and whose net worth was probably no more than $350,000.” [n44] Huntington organized them late in 1860. The core five were Huntington, Leland Stanford, Mark Hopkins and the Crocker brothers (Charles and Edwin). They also built the Dutch Flat toll wagon road as a hedge.
- **¶53:** initial investment was “$1,500 apiece for all of them except Edwin Crocker,” plus $1,500 from James Bailey, so $7,500 was 10% down on 750 shares. Each Associate later bought 345 more shares. White’s estimate of the total combined investment is “$19,800.” Huntington used assessments in 1862 to threaten to force out Bailey and Judah. (Note-number glitch in the ebook: [n48] appears twice; the first should be n46.)
- **¶62:** in seeking the 1864 Act, “Huntington and Durant offered congressmen and others private favors to secure a public bounty. Durant and his allies distributed $250,000 in bonds … This was almost certainly the occasion upon which the Central Pacific gave some of its convertible bonds … to the men Mark Hopkins called ‘our friends of influence.’ [n59: Hopkins to CPH, Feb. 16, 1864, CPH Papers] […]”
- ¶63–65: government bonds were reduced to a **second** mortgage, and the companies were allowed an equal amount of first-mortgage bonds. Courts later held that interest was not due until maturity, so “for thirty years the government, not the railroads, would pay the interest.” White values the gift to the Union Pacific at about $43,000,000 in addition to the $77,000,000 loan.
- **¶66–68 (land):** the 1864 Act doubled the grant to 12,800 acres per mile plus coal and iron. Odd sections formed a checkerboard, and the price of the public sections was doubled to $2.50/acre. “The main line of the Central Pacific got slightly more than the landmass of Maryland.” Federal grants to all land-grant railroads totaled 131,230,358 acres (“Railroadiana”, third-largest state). State grants added 44,224,175 acres (33 million of them from Texas).
- ¶69: the land “belonged to Indians.” The Kickapoo Treaty is the case: Senator Pomeroy steered surplus lands to his own railroad (Twain’s Senator Dilworthy).
- ¶70: delays in issuing patents meant localities “could not easily tax railroad lands.”
- ¶71: the 1864 Act set no meeting point, so construction became “a race for subsidies.”
- ¶75: the March 1865 amendment allowed 100 miles of bonds in advance of construction. The Central Pacific made some of them convertible. The par-payment requirement was “never enforced.”
- **¶76:** under California’s General Railroad Law (stockholder liability and borrowing limited to capital stock), the Central Pacific issued stock “to the construction companies owned by the same men who controlled the Central Pacific … The best estimates are that at most $100,000 was paid for shares of the Central Pacific. The company’s annual report to the secretary of state of California that claimed $40 million in paid stock subscriptions in 1869 was fraudulent.” [n74: 1869 Annual Report, California State Archives]
- ¶77: “rule number one … put little or no money down … rule number two: negotiate among yourselves,” through the insider construction company.
- **¶79:** Charles Crocker & Co. was the “primitive Crédit Mobilier.” Hopkins, Feb. 1866: “[H]aving reached the present position [with] so little [stock] in outside hands, I think now it will pay us best to issue as little as possible & calculate to work it into the contractor & make it bring par or over within three years when the annual earnings of the road will be very large.” [n76: Hopkins to CPH, Feb. 16, 1866]
- **¶80:** the Contract and Finance Company was incorporated in 1867. “Its nominal incorporators actually held stock for the five Associates, who never paid anything for it. Instead, they gave their notes for the stock.” The Pacific Railway Commission estimated the Central Pacific “paid roughly twice the actual cost of construction per mile.” In 1868 the company got half of its $16,512,000 payment in gold and half in Central Pacific stock. Huntington, years later: “as rotten a corporation as ever lived.” [n77: CPH to H. E. Huntington, Jan. 5, 1898]
- **¶81 (Stanford and the Chinese):** “Leland Stanford, the president of the Central Pacific, had tried to exclude Chinese—the ‘dregs of Asia’—from California while governor … Chinese labor proved to be the Central Pacific’s salvation.”
- ¶85–86: Hopkins considered stopping at the California line. They jumped ahead into Nevada for the bonds. E. B. Crocker would “tremble for the future” (Feb. 1867).
- **¶94:** “when threatened with a congressional investigation, the books disappeared, never to resurface.” [n99: PRC 74] By Pacific Railway Commission figures, the bonds “roughly matched the cost of the road … more than enough to build the road without any substantial investment from the Associates.”
- **¶95:** Fleisig: the Associates turned “an actual investment, not all of it theirs, of about $275,000” into a corporation capitalized at $135,346,964 in 1873, more than the Pennsylvania Railroad ($116,658,824). “Real money came from taxpayers through federal, state, and local subsidies, and it came through bonds.” [n100: Fleisig, JEH 35 (1975)]
- ¶96: per the Pacific Railway Commission, the Associates made “a profit of more than $10 million from paying themselves to build the road.”
- **¶97–98 (Stanford):** “In 1870 Leland Stanford made one of his many expensive mistakes when Sam Brannan tried to sell back his 200 shares … at par.” Brannan sued for a full accounting. The Associates bought the stock from Mrs. Brannan after the divorce “at $850 a share, effectively squashing the suit.” Lawyer Silas Sanderson warned that buyers of the stock would gain the right “to call the directors to account.” [n103 cites Stewart v. Huntington depositions of Stanford and Crocker]
- ¶100: Promontory, May 10, 1869. Stanford is described as “ex-grocer, ex–Civil War governor.” Union Pacific 1,032 miles, Central Pacific 881 miles. ¶104: the Hewes spike is dated May 8, not May 10.

## Interlude dm01 “A Railroad Life: H. K. Thomas” (¶1–41)
- This is the 1870–71 diary of a Laramie, Wyoming stationmaster for the Union Pacific: social history of a railroad town. Read in full. It is mostly not Cardinal material.
- ¶12: during the Carbon coal-mine strike, federal troops from Fort Steele came “quickly by railroad.” Management fired the strike leaders and imported Scandinavians. This is an early example of state power on the side of railroad interests.
- ¶21: Stanford is named only as a magnate who appears in the diary.

## Ch. 2 “Annus Horribilis: 1873” (¶1–115)
- ¶3–17: winter blockades of 1869 and 1871–72 shut the Union Pacific and Central Pacific. Rival routes multiplied (Atchison, Atlantic & Pacific, Northern Pacific, Kansas Pacific). Interlocking Boston and New York board networks: “with the exception of the Associates, directors drawn from west of the Missouri … were loosely networked” (¶15). Families used the boards “less to direct the railroads than to manage their investments, culling inside information” (¶17).
- ¶18–41: Mexico and Canada (Rosecrans, Palmer, Plumb; Cooke, Allan, Macdonald). This is peripheral to Cardinal but was read in full.
- ¶43–49: Indian land and the Northern Pacific. Senator William Windom was also a Northern Pacific director who borrowed from Cooke to invest. The House ended treaty-making in 1871 after the Osage “King Joy” sale. In the Wahpeton-Sisseton agreement, Cooke “authorized payments to Assistant Secretary of the Interior B. R. Cowen,” and a commissioner was a Northern Pacific subsidiary official. Pattern: state power was captured for land.
- ¶51–59: the Crédit Mobilier scandal. Dawes’s dividends were itemized. Ames’s defense was “friendship and reciprocity,” not a quid pro quo (¶57). This is White’s “friends” idea.
- ¶64: a German banker inquired into “the responsibility and financial ability” of Huntington, Crocker, Stanford and Hopkins.
- ¶66: in 1872 Speyer & Co. forced the Central Pacific to issue its first stockholders’ report.
- **¶67 (Stanford):** “Either from unusual honesty or from normal carelessness, Leland Stanford listed all the company’s debts, the principal and interest owed the federal government, in his draft of the 1872 Central Pacific annual report.” Huntington: “I shall strike the interest out unless I get the reasons why they were put in as this report is to help the sale of stock and this item of say $80,000,000 is not in that direction.” [n83: CPH to Hopkins, Dec. 19, 1872, Hopkins Collection] This is direct evidence of intent to conceal the federal debt from investors.
- ¶71–73: Simonton of the Associated Press and the *Bulletin* was hostile. Huntington propped up Central Pacific bond prices so the move would be reported by Reuters (Sept. 1873): “an advance of 7/8 … will more than counter balance any political news from Cal.”
- **¶89:** the Central Pacific tried “to get a government grant of Goat Island in San Francisco Bay. If the Associates succeeded, they would create their own port and leave San Francisco isolated and commercially irrelevant.”
- ¶93: Hopkins on William Ralston’s Bank of California “ring”: they “know all about our business here.”
- **¶94:** Huntington offered the Central Pacific (capitalized at $135M and claiming $59,644,000 paid-up stock, “which, of course, it did not”) to Cohen, Reese and the Bank of California ring for $20M, with only $2–4M down. They countered at $11M. [n120: they also offered the Southern Pacific for $15M]
- ¶95: about $2M of interest was due Jan. 1, 1873, while the annual report showed only $2,722,244 for the whole year and apparently omitted the floating debt. Hopkins: “financial dishonor is unavoidable.” [n123]
- **¶96 (Stanford’s character):** “Stanford’s preference in any crisis was to do nothing. Actually, at any time, his preference was to do nothing.” Hopkins: Stanford “will be condemned for leaving undone those things that he might have done.” [n124: Hopkins to CPH, Oct. 10, 1872] Stanford intercepted crisis telegrams meant for Huntington. Huntington: it “was not … safe to do business with him or in other words trust him to do anything.” [n125: CPH to Hopkins, Jan. 3, 1873]
- ¶101: Huntington advised Stanford to “retire and enjoy” their gains, even selling the Central Pacific at half its worth [n132: CPH to Stanford, Feb. 28, 1873]. The Crockers had left, and Charles returned in 1873 when the others could not pay for his share.
- ¶100, ¶103–106: Cooke was rescued in 1872 through Treasury friends. He failed on Sept. 18, 1873, triggering the Panic of 1873.
- ¶112: Huntington let the Chesapeake & Ohio fail, which brought down Fisk & Hatch. The Central Pacific survived because Fisk & Hatch’s creditors agreed to stay quiet.
- **¶113:** “Everyone agreed to lie.” The 1873 annual report said “the financial and business prospects of your Company were never brighter.” Meanwhile Hopkins said it was “impossible” to pay the January interest and planned to pay it by not paying workers or taxes, “robbing Peter to pay Paul.” [n148]
- ¶115: Hassler: the “concealment of truth” was the main cause, and the country would be “better off without” some lines.

## Interlude dm02 “A Railroad Life: William Hyde” (¶1–15)
- Hyde was an engineer and lobbyist; his letters are in the Stanford University Archives. Terminal Central Pacific / Goat Island (Yerba Buena) scheme. Huntington on Goat Island: “I went to see the President yesterday … and had a very full talk with him.” [n5: CPH to Hopkins, May 11, 1872]
- ¶12: Hyde on buying “Spanish element” votes in a Los Angeles subsidy election. This is a racist source, quoted by White.
- **¶13:** “At the very moment Hyde wrote, Huntington was floating a complicated scheme to Stanford and Mark Hopkins that involved bribing enough people to control the San Francisco board of supervisors, having San Francisco vote a bond issue to buy a rival to the Southern Pacific that would secretly be controlled by Huntington, Hopkins, and Stanford, and then using the bonds […]” [n16: CPH to Hopkins, Sept. 14, 18, 1872. “Huntington originally proposed a $2 million bribe, but decided $1 million would suffice.”] This was proposed, not shown to have been carried out.
- ¶15: in 1872 Huntington, Stanford and Crocker were trying to sell the Southern Pacific to Tom Scott.

## Ch. 3 “Friends” (¶1–113) — CORE: lobby, bribery and Stanford’s reputation among the Associates
- Epigraph: Huntington, “[W]e must take care of our friends.” [n1: CPH to Hopkins, March 19, 1872]
- ¶3: the Associates’ letters read “Friend Huntington, Friend Stanford, Friend Crocker, and Friend Hopkins.”
- ¶7: the Associates bought the Southern Pacific (a paper road) and moved its route to the Central Valley as a “night watchman” for the Central Pacific monopoly. “they bought virtually everything that ran on two rails in California.”
- ¶13–15, press control: free passes, printing contracts and loans to newspapermen. Franchot put the AP’s Washington reporter on the Central Pacific payroll. In 1875 the Associates tried to buy exclusive AP membership for the *Chronicle*, which they then controlled. By 1883 they were subsidizing the *Bulletin*, the *Call* and country papers. Huntington to Colton: “Capital is always timid.”
- **¶19 (Stanford, how the Associates saw him):** “Friend Huntington despised Friend Stanford.” Huntington to C. Crocker, 1871: “Stanford will go to work for the railroad company as soon as the horse races are over.” [n16] Huntington to Stanford: “I wish you would tell me whom to correspond with in Cal. when I want anything done; for I have become thoroughly convinced that there is no use in writing to you.” [n17: CPH to Stanford, Oct. 25, 1871] “Their writing on Stanford is a chronicle of amazement, dismay, and irritation at his greed, laziness, ignorance, and ineptitude.” [n18] Hopkins: “He could do it … but not without more mental effort than is agreeable to him.” [n19: Dec. 8, 1872] Ex-Senator Conness called Stanford “this immensely stupid man.” [n20: Conness to CPH, Nov. 1875]
- ¶20: David Colton (embezzler) said “we have got no true friends outside of us five.”
- ¶22: Stanford addressed workers as friends “engaged in a common enterprise” with a “common bond of sympathy.” [n23]
- ¶23: “Destroy when read” appears throughout railroad–politician letters.
- **¶26, the rebate slush fund:** Huntington demanded 2½–5% rebates from suppliers, paid in cash, which became “a slush fund … ‘payments for which it was thought just as well not to take any receipts’” (William Mahl). [n28: Testimony of Leland Stanford, Aug. 10, 1887, PRC 6:3161–200; Mahl memoir]
- **¶27:** while Senator William Stewart of Nevada chaired the Pacific Railroad Committee, “the Central Pacific gave him fifty thousand acres of land in the San Joaquin Valley, the whole transaction disguised with a dummy trustee.” [n29: Lavender; CPH to Hopkins, Dec. 30, 1871] Senator Barnum: “I bought the 1000 wheels of Barnum … He is in the U.S. Senate and does about what I want.” [n30]
- ¶28: Conkling’s $60,000 invested with Huntington grew to $84,000 in 1880 on inside advice. [n31]
- **¶29:** “William Carr … a political fixer and an intimate of Stanford,” controlled federal patronage in San Francisco. He received $60,000 in Southern Pacific bonds by 1875, and a further $10–20,000 a year was considered. [n32]
- ¶31: the lobby “made politics a realm of economic competition between corporations.”
- ¶38: Huntington, 1876: “35 bills in the senate and the house that we are interested in.”
- ¶41: the Central Pacific hired ex-congressmen, including S. C. Pomeroy, Julian and Trumbull, plus Boyd (an assistant House doorkeeper) and Gorham (made secretary of the Senate).
- ¶47: Hopkins, 1873: “Congress and Legislatures were gentle steeds. Bless me how they rear and tear now.” Huntington, 1871: “Some of us ought to act with the Democratic party … the five loaves and two fishes.”
- ¶52–55: antimonopoly definitions: special privilege, discrimination, and the private “tax.”
- **¶58 (Stanford in politics):** “When Stanford tried to defeat Booth’s run for the U.S. Senate, he turned to the Democrats. Booth not only won, but Stanford undercut the pro-railroad Republican machine and alienated the railroad’s important friends, Senator Sargent and George Gorham.” The 1879 constitution and the 1882 Stoneman election went against the railroad. Sherrill, 1886: California gave “more opposition … than … any other State.”
- ¶59: Charles Crocker said the railroad learned to “manage men after the election.” Luttrell was first called “a wild hog” (“Beat him”) and later “an honest man.”
- ¶61–64: Congressman E. J. Ellis was on an annual retainer from Huntington, of which 80% had been paid. He would serve Huntington’s “interests in any way shape, manner and form.”
- ¶65: Huntington: when the corrupt are in power “bribery may be the last and only means left to honest men.”
- ¶66: Huntington on Senator Stewart: “we must fix it so that he can make one or two hundred thousand dollars.” [n89: CPH to Crocker, May 17, 1869]
- **¶68 (Stanford asked to pay Conkling):** Huntington “once asked Stanford to ‘arrange something out of which he [Conkling] could make some money (something handsome). You will have to be very careful how you do it, as he is very sensitive, but, of course, like the rest of us, has to eat and drink.’” [n92: CPH to Stanford, May 26, 1869, Huntington Letters 1:433; Octopus Speaks 145]
- ¶69: Senator Cole’s “loans”: Huntington did not sue because it could “open up some questions that would possibly hurt us.”
- ¶77: in 1874 the Central Pacific carried 6,186 “deadheads” (free-pass riders).
- ¶81: Huntington: “[W]ith a certain sum of money … I can stop almost any legislation when the same amount will not pass the bill.”
- ¶85–87: Scott’s World deal with Manton Marble ($100,000 contingent on a subsidy). Huntington paid Platt $5,000. Harvey Brown of the Central Pacific spent $9,000 with a “certain party,” allegedly including $5,000 for Lamar to delay a report. Brown denied it.
- ¶95: Scott and the 1876 Hayes election. White says Woodward’s claim that Scott won Hayes the presidency is “discredited.”
- ¶96: Windom took $1,500 from the Southern Pacific lobby (“hold his receipt for same”), Feb. 1878. Senator Ferry drew $10,000 as a “loan.”
- ¶98: in 1878 the California Railroad Commission disclosed the Associates’ large floating debt, which Scott publicized.
- ¶99: Frémont, as Arizona territorial governor, asked the Southern Pacific for $2,500.
- **¶101:** John Boyd’s March 1879 expense report: “$125 × 85 … There seems only one likely explanation for a single notation repeated 85 times: the cost of a vote.” [n150: Boyd to CPH, March 5, 1879] This is White’s inference.
- **¶102 (unvouchered money):** the 1887 Pacific Railway Commission found “$4,818,355.67 on the books of the Central Pacific Railroad for which there were insufficient vouchers.” Commission: “There is no room for doubt … that a large portion of this money was used for the purpose of influencing legislation … and for the purpose of influencing elections.” By comparison: federal taxes $29,812.54, legal expenses $2,361,154.00, state and local taxes $5,857,380. [n151: PRC Report 84, tables 29, 31] Stanford was the Central Pacific’s president throughout.
- ¶105: delaying land patents to avoid local taxes: “Almost 90 percent of the Central Pacific’s land in Nevada and Utah was patented only after 1893.” [n155: Kincaid diss. 1922]
- ¶106: when forfeiture threatened, the railroads reversed and asked for patents.
- ¶107–111: the Thurman Act (1878, sinking fund). Booth on the Gordon substitute: “the coin and mintage of their brains.” The Burlington memo: Union Pacific stock “never contributed a dollar to the construction of the property.”
- ¶113: Huntington told the 1887 commission that the Washington money was to bring “moral influence” to bear.
- (Citation note: n152 cites “Testimony of Leland Stanford, Aug. 5, 1884, PRC 5:2949.” The Pacific Railway Commission sat in 1887, so “1884” looks like a typo in White’s notes. Cite PRC vol./page only.)

## Interlude dm03 “A Railroad Life: Elias C. Boudinot” (¶1–16)
- Boudinot was a Cherokee booster for the Atlantic & Pacific and the KATY, and Vinita’s townsite promoter. Frémont’s railroad frauds (convicted in absentia in Paris; about $6M taken from small investors). Land grants depended on the extinction of Indian title: the KATY’s 4,121,600 acres were “subject to temporary Indian occupancy” (¶8). Read in full. **Off-topic for Cardinal** (Indian Territory, no Stanford, Central Pacific or California link), apart from the general land-grant-as-collateral pattern (¶9: bonds were sold in Europe on grants that were in abeyance).

## Ch. 4 “Spatial Politics” (¶1–109) — California rates, urban land, Octopus, Santa Clara
- ¶1–36: a conceptual frame. Absolute space (track, survey grid) versus relational space (time and cost). The tariff turns distance into money, and the instability of rates is the core of antimonopoly politics. Canadian Pacific and Van Horne examples. Read in full; background.
- ¶40: Central Pacific freight cars grew from 3,200 to 5,850 between the Civil War and 1900.
- ¶44–45: insider fast-freight lines “skimmed the cream” of traffic, and refrigerator-car lines did the same.
- ¶46–49: insiders profited from knowing where stations and division points would go: Hill’s Northwest Land Co., Perkins, Billings. Adams: “town site schemes are jobs from beginning to end.”
- **¶50:** “The Associates failed in their efforts to overcome San Francisco and control the juncture of rail and shipping on the Pacific Coast by creating at either Goat Island or Oakland a port/terminal where they owned all the surrounding real estate.” Los Angeles also defeated the Southern Pacific’s Santa Monica port plan.
- **¶52 (urban land values):** the Associates got 60 acres at Mission Bay. The Central Pacific valued its 12 million acres of grant land at $30.37M, but its half share of Mission Bay tideland at $285,000 ($9,487/acre): “1 acre in San Francisco equaled 3,795 acres of land outside San Francisco.” The annual report valued 670 urban acres (Sacramento, Oakland, Mission Bay) at $7,750,000, which is “roughly 25 percent of the entire value of the rest of its land grant.” [n65: Cal. Board of Transportation Commissioners Report, Dec. 1877, 291–93; n66: Central Pacific Annual Report 1878, p. 4 and p. 47: 11,722,400 acres valued at $29,306,000]
- ¶58–60: the railroads did not know their own costs. Mahl, 1889, on the Southern Pacific books: four auditors, a “constant changing in methods … horrorful in every way.”
- **¶64:** Lathrop, “the maiden name of Jane Stanford,” was “the most important junction on the C.P. system.” [n84: Albert Wilbur letters, Wilbur Papers, Beinecke]
- **¶65–69:** the Southern Pacific bought out the San Joaquin steamboats and set rates to keep wheat off the river. Farmers paid at most $6/ton to ship wheat out but up to $25/ton for goods coming in. “the railroad had erased the San Joaquin as a source of transportation not because of superior technology or efficiency but because it used its rates to manipulate and control space.”
- ¶70: the 1883 Railroad Commission cut grain rates by 35%, but only for grain going east, “not the direction that grain traveled.”
- **¶71:** Nevada rates from Ogden were 3–6 times the San Francisco-origin rates. “In 1881, when the Associates had managed to make friends of two of the three railroad commissioners, the California Railroad Commission ruled” such rates acceptable. [n92]
- ¶72–76: the Union Pacific and Central Pacific paid the Pacific Mail Steamship Co. to keep its rates up. In 1880 the subsidy rose from $25,000 to $110,000 a month, and Gould and Huntington joined its board. “It could make more money by doing less.”
- ¶78–79: San Francisco wholesalers and the railroad reached an accommodation “on the backs of interior consumers.” Norris’s *Octopus* Bonneville scene.
- ¶80–82: the special contract system: half rates only for merchants who shipped all their goods by rail, with the railroad inspecting their books. Commissioner Foote: it “partakes more of the nature of a crime than a mere breach of duty.” Two commissioners elected in 1882 “were widely rumored to have become friends of the railroad.”
- **¶85 (Santa Clara and Field):** the Southern Pacific “had nearly crippled many counties by refusing to pay taxes.” *County of San Mateo v. SPRR* (1882) and *County of Santa Clara v. SPRR* (1883) “voided the tax bills … and ruled that the corporation was a person within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment.” [n109: 13 Fed. 145 (July 31, 1882); 18 Fed. 385 (Sept. 17, 1883). These are the **circuit court** decisions, not the 1886 Supreme Court opinion.] Stephen Mallory White called Field “one of the most dishonest characters that has ever discharged the function of a judicial office.” Field was “one of the Associates’ most reliable judicial friends.” [n110]
- ¶86–87: Governor Stoneman’s 1884 special session on taxes and the Barry bill.
- **¶88 (Stanford and Buckley):** the antimonopoly bills died on tie votes in the state senate. Nine of ten San Francisco Democratic senators (Buckley’s “lambs”) voted against them. “The Pacific Railway Commission later estimated that the Southern Pacific spent about $700,000 on politics that year.” “**The political marriage of Leland Stanford and Christopher Buckley gave birth to the Octopus.**” [n113: Williams doubts corruption, while McAfee “presents a very strong case that it did”; Issel & Cherny] The historians disagree, and White flags this.
- ¶89–92: the Santa Fe and Atlantic & Pacific arrived in 1885 and rate wars followed. The Pacific Mail went after sugar (Spreckels), salmon and wine.
- ¶93: the Traffic Association of California (1891) was a merchants’ “union.”
- ¶99–108: pools, rebates and secret rates. Larrabee: “much railroad stock represented no real capital investment and … much of the actual capital had come from public subsidies in the West.”

## Interlude dm04 “A Railroad Life: Alfred A. Cohen” (¶1–21) — STANFORD
- ¶4: Cohen sold the Associates the Oakland Railroad and Ferry (1868) and the Alameda Railroad and Ferry (1869). These were consolidated with the Central Pacific in 1870.
- ¶5: “In the early 1870s Huntington had considered making Cohen an Associate, and Stanford seemed to agree, but in his usual bungling manner, he changed his mind.” Huntington called Cohen “Israelite friend” behind his back, an antisemitic slur White records.
- ¶8–11: Cohen lobbied for the Archer rate bill, allied in practice with Scott. Cohen’s Dec. 1876 pamphlet-play *The California King* mocked Stanford as “sullen, remorseless, grand, and peculiar…with the ambition of an Emperor and the spite of a peanut vendor.”

## Ch. 5 “Kilkenny Cats” (¶1–117) — CORE: Colton trial, WDC, sinking funds, selling the Central Pacific to Europeans
- ¶6–40: Charles Francis Adams became Union Pacific president in 1884. Gould and the Kansas Pacific merger of 1880: the Union Pacific took on $39M in debt and the Pacific Railway Commission accountants said a “ring … managed the business with highest skill and cunning.” Gould’s 1887 “scalped conductor” testimony about risk (ties actually cost 65–90 cents, not $5–10). Overcapitalization comes from “fraud, deception, and insider dealing” more than risk (¶21). Read in full; Union Pacific material is context.
- **¶22–23:** Commissioner E. Ellery Anderson questioning Stanford, July 26, 1887: “it seems to be the general belief that the present weak condition of the Central Pacific … is due to the fact that the contract with Crocker & Co., and the contract with the Contract and Finance Company, and the contracts with the Western Development Company, and the contracts with the Pacific Improvement Company have drained the company of its […]” [n21: PRC 5:2630]
- **¶42:** Huntington claimed the builders “profited very little,” then complained that Stanford, Hopkins and Crocker had taken out “something over five millions to build three dwelling houses for the communists and agrarians to look at.” [n52: CPH to W. W. Stow, Feb. 8, 1893] “The Associates did not make money from the Central Pacific or the Southern Pacific, although these were the basis of their fortunes; they made money from the Contract and Finance Company, the Western Development Company, and the Pacific Improvement Company.” In 1877 banks still required the Associates’ personal notes [n53].
- ¶43–44: the Colton trial opened at Santa Rosa on Nov. 14, 1883. Ellen Colton sued Stanford, Huntington, Crocker and the Hopkins estate. David Colton had embezzled (the Rocky Mountain Coal and Iron check).
- **¶45:** the Western Development Co., successor of the Contract and Finance Co., “concealed, transformed, and transferred assets so that debts incurred by the Associates’ properties could end up as money in the pockets of the Associates. The Associates never paid for their stock in the WDC … The WDC was a screen to obscure the activities of the men who ran the Central Pacific and Southern Pacific from bondholders and bankers.” [n56]
- ¶46: Colton’s lawyers: “The corporations were the individuals, and the individuals were the corporations.” [n57: Colton Case Testimony 3:1002]
- **¶47–48 (sinking-fund self-dealing):** from 1872 the Central Pacific lent its federal-debt **sinking fund** to the WDC at 10%. The loans went into the Associates’ individual accounts. As collateral, in 1875 the Associates transferred 17,577 Central Pacific shares from their WDC accounts. By 1879 the WDC had borrowed over $3M from the sinking funds, and “Most of the debt was settled by letting the Central Pacific keep the collateral.” Huntington to Colton: “what we have done is not a thing to talk about, and I do not think there is a careful business man in the world outside of our five selves who would say it was well invested, while we know it is.” [n60: CPH to Colton, Nov. 16, 1877; Colton Case Testimony]
- ¶49: the Associates’ attorney McAllister admitted it “may be a transaction open to censure by proper parties.” Ellen Colton lost.
- ¶50: J. O’B. Gunn found “John Miller” (really Ambrose Woodruff) had stolen $300,000, and up to $900,000 was missing. The railroad witnesses cleared Miller because “Miller/Woodruff knew too much.”
- **¶51:** U.S. railroad auditor Theophilus French (1879) found the Central Pacific borrowed “money, ostensibly for investment in branch lines, steel rails, &c.,” but “really for payment of dividends to stockholders.” “French never pushed his concerns. The Central Pacific put him on the payroll.” [n64–65]
- **¶52:** in 1880 Huntington began creating a market among European investors. $5M in new stock went into the dividend fund: “It was something of a Ponzi scheme.” The stock reached 105½ in 1881.
- **¶53–54:** by 1883 the Associates had sold their majority of the Central Pacific. Europeans left the shares in the names of the Associates’ dummies, so the Associates kept proxy control. Huntington: “while we can prattle about the road and its business, we need not say anything more than our credit requires.” [n69: CPH to Crocker, April 5, 1883] In 1885 the lease was reversed (the Southern Pacific leased the Central Pacific for 99 years). The dividend was halved for 1884, then eliminated until 1888, and the stock fell to 26. “The British were sheep the Associates sheared not once but twice.”
- ¶56: traffic was diverted from the Central Pacific to the Southern Pacific’s Sunset Route. Central Pacific earnings fell 40% in 1884 ($8,094,149 to $4,872,734), and by spring 1886 the Sunset Route carried 93.7% of California–New York rail freight. This also reduced Central Pacific payments into the Thurman sinking fund. [n73]
- ¶62: Huntington: “very few people on this side know how little business there is there.”
- ¶66: the Pacific Improvement Company, “their latest corrupt corporate jack-of-all-trades,” built the Galveston, Harrisburg & San Antonio extension.
- ¶69: Canadian Pacific parallels (land grant for debt; cutting upkeep to “make a good showing”).
- ¶72–88: Kansas overbuilding and Harley’s model. Huntington on the Santa Fe’s Sonora Railroad: as long as it could “float securities so as to build railroads at a profit, their paying or non-paying after completion has very little to do with their building.”
- **¶90 (Mexico):** Huntington sent Díaz adviser John Frisbie Jr. (“already on Huntington’s payroll”) $25,000, part of which went to Federal District governor Ramón Fernández. Construction-company stock went to Limantour (finance minister), Romero Rubio (Díaz’s father-in-law), Fernández, and the wife of Governor Treviño. [n147 includes CPH to Stanford, Feb. 2, 1881]
- ¶94–112: Villard and the Northern Pacific (the Blind Pool; completion in 1883 as illusion). ¶114–117: receivership kept insolvent roads alive as “corporate zombies” (Wabash, 1884).

- **¶106:** “To pay Stanford’s debts and to satisfy his bequests, the Stanford estate needed money. Jane Stanford announced her plans to withdraw from the railroad business and liquidate her assets.” Huntington: “If we all stand together I think we shall get through.” [n157–158]
- **¶107:** Huntington proposed stripping the PIC’s best assets into the Southern Pacific for 10-year notes. He sold Crocker’s collateral stock, and **“Undeterred, Huntington also sold stock belonging to the Stanford estate.”** [n164: H. Spencer to CPH, Aug. 23, 1893]
- **¶108:** “Jane Stanford could sink the railroad and the PIC by withdrawing her quarter share; to stop her, Huntington mortgaged virtually everything the PIC owned.” By March 1894 there were “virtually no unhypothecated securities” in the estate’s name [n165]. **Olney “prepared a suit against the Stanford estate to recover the loans to the Central Pacific … Because California incorporation law made individual stockholders responsible for corporate debts, Jane and her new university were now vulnerable.”** [n166: Olney to Hoadly, May 19, June 19, 1894; Olney to Hoar, June 8, 1894] (Outcome: *United States v. Stanford*, 161 U.S. 412 (1896), government lost. Web-verify.)
- ¶109: by April 1894 the PIC owed over $16M.
- ¶112–119: in Nov./Dec. 1893 the Central Pacific lease was renegotiated to zero rent. Bretherton called it “a mere naked fraud” and illegal because of common directors. He said the Associates were “contracting with themselves,” the report falsified income and dropped the floating debt, and the sinking fund had been looted. [n176–189]
- ¶116: Speyer knew “the way in which C.P. sinking funds have been invested or allowed to be used for purposes contrary to the spirit and the conditions laid down in the mortgages.”
- ¶121: April 1894: “the mountain labored and brought forth a mouse.” **By early May, Jane Stanford was once more refusing to sign notes.** [n197]
- ¶122: Huntington took a $2.5M personal loan from E. H. Harriman.
- ¶123: in Aug. 1896 the Speyers rescued the Southern Pacific and PIC, taking everything as security, including a mortgage on Huntington’s mansion, with independent accountants on the books.
- **¶124:** “By the end of 1897 Jane Stanford and the Crockers, discouraged and exhausted, were ready to sell out, but Huntington did not have the funds to buy.”

## Interlude dm09 “Mise en scène: Reading the Newspapers” (¶1–11)
- ¶3: as Buckley fell in 1891, “Denunciation of the bosses morphed easily into denunciations of the Buckley’s old ally Senator Leland Stanford, which did not displease Collis P. Huntington.” The *Bulletin* and *Call* “were tools of the railroad” and attacked the railroad and Stanford to look independent. [n2: Mills to CPH, Dec. 15, 1891]
- ¶4: Fred Crocker cut the railroad’s newspaper subsidies; Huntington restored them.
- ¶7: Huntington forgave de Young “the role he had played in helping defeat A. A. Sargent and elect Leland Stanford” and thought de Young could pass the funding bill, “something Stanford could not do.” [n8–9]
- **¶8:** Mills negotiated $1,000/month for 30 months for a treaty of “amity and comity between the Company” and Hearst’s *Examiner*. The *Examiner* would stop criticizing the Southern Pacific on important issues but keep up minor criticism “to retain the sympathy of the public.” Huntington vetoed it, then approved a modified form. [n11: Mills to CPH, June 16, 1892] This is the railroad buying the press.
- ¶9: the *Bulletin* and *Call* “apparently went over to Crocker and Stanford.” Mills: “Unnamed friends of Stanford were using the press to attack Huntington’s management.”
- ¶10: Stephen White accused George Hearst of buying his Senate seat, “which was true.”

## Ch. 10 “Strike” (¶1–111): ARU and the Pullman Strike, with major Southern Pacific/Sacramento content and **the Jane Stanford train**
- ¶3–21: Debs’s liberal antimonopolism; corporations as legal persons. ¶14: Attorney General Olney remained on the Burlington payroll (and seemingly the Atchison’s) while in office [n20]. Olney, “supposedly influenced by Stephen Field … old friend of the Associates,” opposed Barry Baldwin as U.S. marshal in San Francisco [n22].
- ¶22–48: the ARU’s rise; the Union Pacific injunction fight (Dundy overruled by Caldwell); the Great Northern strike, won by arbitration in 1894. The Maxwell opinion that any train with a mail car is a mail train (¶43).
- ¶49–63: Pullman boycott, Northern Pacific details.
- ¶64–71: California. Sacramento shops: 2,000 walked out on June 29, joining 1,000 others. Mills edited the Southern Pacific house paper, the *Sacramento Record-Union*, by telegraph and steered the country press. Henry E. Huntington privately: “We are going to break this strike.” Collis wanted to purge all union men gradually. Mills: Hearst “was in the pay of the Southern Pacific” yet favored the strikers. The Record-Union vs. the Chronicle was the “difference between a wife and a harlot.”
- ¶76: U.S. Marshal Baldwin sympathized with the strikers. U.S. Attorney Denis in Los Angeles sought an injunction against the Southern Pacific under the Sherman Act.
- ¶78–84: Olney coordinated with the General Managers’ Association. Edwin Walker was appointed special counsel. Injunctions; federal troops in Chicago.
- **¶86 (Jane Stanford train):** “On July 3 the ARU allowed one train through. The train carried Jane Stanford from her ranch in Dunsimore, and it was guarded by ARU men and decorated with flags … It was a slap at Collis P. Huntington and the present management of the railroad, **which Jane Stanford hated far more than she did the ARU.** Her disdain was […]” [n155: *SF Examiner*, July 4, 1894, pp. 2, 4; n156: Southern Pacific Journal of incidents … ARU Strike, June 27–Aug. 31, p. 20, HM 55579, Huntington Library]. VERIFY “Dunsimore”: probably Dunsmuir (Siskiyou Co.). The Stanfords’ big northern ranch was Vina (Tehama Co.). White’s place name may be an error; check the Examiner text.
- ¶87–94: Sacramento, July 3–11: the crowd uncoupled the Pullmans and Guard units fraternized with strikers. On July 11 a trestle was sabotaged near the Yolo bridge; engineer Sam Clarke and four soldiers were killed and the strike was crushed by July 16.
- ¶102: 132 California strike leaders were prosecuted for conspiracy. The prosecutor H. S. Foote was “always our friend” (Huntington); Henry Huntington: “I suppose we will have to do something for Mr. Foote … ‘how much?’” [n176–177] The trial ended in a hung jury.
- ¶103: the Southern Pacific said the strike cost it $545,000 but recouped the loss through payroll cuts.
- **¶107:** the Southern Pacific needed the funding bill (debts due from 1895; risk of foreclosure on the Central Pacific) and a federal port at Santa Monica, which it controlled. Huntington did favors and handed out passes. **“Collis asked his nephew Henry not to number his political letters, because Collis was destroying them, and he did not want traces in the record.”** [n184: CPH to HEH, Sept. 5, 1894] Pair with ch6 n98 (Stanford records probably destroyed by Jane): deliberate archival gaps on both sides.
- ¶109: in Aug. 1895 Henry Huntington authorized $2,000/month to Utah Senator Arthur Brown. [n189]
- ¶110–111: Huntington lost both the harbor fight and the funding fight. Bierce: “Of our modern Forty Thieves, Mr. Huntington is the surviving 36.” The Pacific railroad debts were settled in 1899 [n193: Meyer, QJE 1899].

## Interlude dm10 “Mise en scène: Following the Detectives” (¶1–8)
- Huntington had a detective put on Mayor Adolph Sutro (1896). Southern Pacific officials put detectives on each other (Fillmore on Stillman and Curtis) and on the Huntingtons themselves (Oct. 1895). Overheard on the street: “There is the old S.[O.]B. He owns the earth and the people here.” [n1–3: HEH Collection] Shows Southern Pacific factional surveillance. Peripheral; no Stanford, land or University content.

## Ch. 11 “Creative Destruction” (¶1–107): settlement, bison, cattle, the Dakotas, social costs — **LAND chapter, key insider-land passages ¶5–7, ¶13, ¶106**
- **¶5:** “Henry George … would have been more correct if he had noted that **insiders within the corporations, not the corporations themselves, had skimmed the best lands and townsites for speculative gain.**” [n3: Orsi, *Sunset Limited*, 79–81] The railroad corporation wanted small freeholders; the insiders speculated. This is the general thesis linking Associates and land.
- **¶6:** speculators had taken the best San Joaquin Valley land from 1867, so after the 1871 route change the Southern Pacific got lieu lands. Among the largest buyers was “E. H. Miller Jr., an old partner of Mark Hopkins, Central Pacific board member, and secretary of the railroad.” Huntington called such men “land thieves,” but “both the railroad and the Associates, acting as individual speculators, bought land from them.” [n4: CPH to Stanford, Dec. 16, 1871; Stanford to CPH, Sept. 30, 1871; n5: Igler; CPH to Hopkins, Feb. 4, 1870; Orsi 114–16] **This is direct evidence that the Associates speculated individually in land along their own grant.**
- **¶7:** when the Central Pacific acquired the Western Pacific, the land grant went to Charles McLaughlin for a token payment [n6: McLaughlin bought 78,369 ac in Alameda, 64,622 ac in Sacramento, 51,574.55 ac in Stanislaus and 133,223 ac in San Joaquin; McAllister 1939 diss. 255–56, 262–63]. **“The second-largest purchaser of Western Pacific Railroad lands in Alameda County was William B. Carr, ‘Boss Billy,’ one of Leland Stanford’s political fixers, who also acted as an intermediary in a huge sale of Southern Pacific lands.”** **This ties a Stanford political operative directly to railroad-grant land.** (Ch3 ¶29: Carr, an “intimate of Stanford,” got $60k in bonds.)
- ¶8–12: railroads aimed migration like “a fire hose.” Mills (1891): the Sacramento Valley lost 20,000 of its natural increase, 1880–90.
- **¶13:** Stanford before the PRC (1887): “It [the Central Pacific] was the first enterprise anywhere in the world which made possible the habitation of regions of country far remote from navigable waters, and has added untold millions to the wealth of the nation.” [n15: Stanford testimony, July 28, 1887, **PRC 7:2465**] **CITATION INCONSISTENCY:** ch8 n106 gives Stanford’s July 28 start as **PRC 5:2461**; volume 5 vs 7 for nearly the same page. Log it. Mills (Dec. 1893): the Central Pacific, California & Oregon, and Oregon & California had disposed of only 20% of their grants, with **12,610,512 acres remaining of 15,653,000 granted**. [n17: Mills to CPH, Dec. 22, 1893]
- ¶17–31: bison slaughter as “dumb growth.” The Northern Pacific’s hide traffic was about 1/3 of 1% of total. Off-topic for Cardinal; read in full.
- ¶32–72: the open-range cattle industry (book counts, the Great Die-Up of 1886–87, illegal fencing of the public domain). ¶48: “It seemed no more necessary for the promoters of cattle corporations to know anything about raising cattle than for Leland Stanford or Collis P. Huntington to know anything about railroads.” Otherwise off-topic.
- ¶73–86: the North vs. South Dakota “laboratory”: unsubsidized roads produced denser, faster settlement, “a compelling argument against land grants.” This is relevant to Cardinal framing on the cost of land grants.
- ¶87–99: “rain follows the plow,” the 1889 Sioux land cession, the Canadian Pacific. ¶96: “in California, the Southern Pacific encouraged farmers to turn to fruit.”
- ¶101–102: White disagrees with Mercer’s social-return estimates for the Central Pacific and Union Pacific. Mills noted that speculators and higher prices slowed settlement.
- ¶103–105: real per-capita income from labor in much of the West in 1900 was below 1880.
- **¶106:** “Collis P. Huntington died in 1900 with an estate valued at roughly $50 million … Huntington’s was only a quarter share of the Southern Pacific Railroad fortune. **Assuming that the families of the other Associates had done as well, they had collectively accumulated $200 million.** Were the Associates and the railroads they controlled worth this?” [n149: Thorpe, *H. E. Huntington*, 157] Note this is White’s extrapolation (“Assuming”), not a measured Stanford figure.

## Interlude dm11 “Mise en scène: Wovoka” (¶1–8)
- Currier & Ives’ *Across the Continent* (1868). In 1890 Lakota and other Ghost Dance delegates rode the Oregon Short Line and the **Central Pacific** across Nevada to Wovoka. “Pueblos helped build the Southern Pacific.” Indigenous context; no Stanford content. Peripheral.

## Epilogue (¶1–21)
- The Civil War generation built railroads “whose accounts would never bear examining.” Adams and Villard skipped their railroad lives in their memoirs.
- **¶8:** Huntington’s 1899 letter to James Speyer and his Nordhoff sketch were attempts “to settle old scores, particularly with his Associate Leland Stanford, whom he regarded as a burden and an embarrassment.” (This is the source behind ch6 n115/n134/n136: CPH to Speyer, Dec. 6, 1899.)
- ¶9: Huntington “cited as proof of his virtue his victories in litigation, as dubious a proof in nineteenth-century America as it is possible to imagine.”
- ¶13: Huntington died in 1900, leaving his fortune to Arabella, her son and his nephew Henry, the source of the Huntington Library.
- ¶20: Adams: successful men were “a set of mere money-getters and traders.”

## Conclusion (¶1–26)
- **¶6:** “Profit arose more from financial markets and insider contracts than from successfully selling transportation.”
- ¶7: modernity as “the triumph of the unfit, whose survival demanded the intervention of the state, which the corporations themselves corrupted.”
- ¶10–13: railroads and the state were “coproductions.” Congress picked winners and losers through land grants and rescissions, investigations, sinking funds, taxes, rights of way. “Politics still offers a way for corporations to compete.”
- ¶22: the “tax on trade”: “A shipper in 1885 was still paying for the frauds of the 1860s.”
- **¶23:** “the dimmer lights among them, such as Leland Stanford, had to have so much explained to them.”
- ¶26: the transcontinentals should not have been built “when and where they were built.”
