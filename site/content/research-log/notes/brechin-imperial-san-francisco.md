## Preface “The Urban Maelstrom” (pp. xxix–xxxiv) and Introduction “New Romes for a New World” (pp. 1–9)
- Thesis (p. xxxi–xxxii): the city “does nothing of its own accord … it is driven.” “Linked dynastic elites … through their control of information” make city and contado “collective tools to perpetuate and expand its wealth and power … they can all agree that the city must grow—and its land values rise—to assure the continuation of their dominion.” The “Pyramid of Mining” is introduced (p. xxxii). **Frame for Cardinal: land values + information control + dynastic elites.**
- p. xxxi: *contado* = the territory a city dominates and draws upon. SF land area ≤47 sq mi since 1856, population never above 800,000 (p. xxxiii).
- Intro pp. 1–9: Reagan’s 1985 second inaugural vs. the acid-rain decay of Washington’s monuments. Leutze’s *Westward the Course of Empire Takes Its Way* (Capitol). Frémont named “Chrysopylae (golden gate)” after Byzantium’s Golden Horn (p. 5). George Berkeley’s 1726 line; Seward 1850 (“the perpetual unity of the Empire”); Perry 1856 (p. 7). C. B. Watson 1920: the “star of empire” only “fill[ed] the coffers of the already overrich” (p. 9, n4). Peripheral as facts; useful as rhetoric. ## Ch. 1 “The Pyramid of Mining” (pp. 13–70)
- pp. 13–16: Pioneer Monument (Thanksgiving 1894) paid for by the James Lick bequest (Lick: “real estate magnate,” fortune “in urban real estate,” p. 15). Irving M. Scott (Union Iron Works) opened the ceremony. The trustees moved the mining figure ahead of agriculture. “Commerce follows the flag … but the flag follows the pick” (p. 15–16).
- pp. 16–22: Mumford’s “Megamachine” as a pyramid. Base: mechanization, metallurgy, militarism, moneymaking. Apex: mining (p. 19). SF motto “Gold in Peace, Iron in War” (p. 16). Classical precedents (Laurium, Rio Tinto: 40,000 slaves; *damnatio ad metalla*). Context only.
- pp. 22–25: Fuggers. The financier who bribed the Imperial Electors for Charles V. **[IVORY]** p. 24–25: “Rothschilds … Rockefellers and Guggenheims developed their own global reach from the new Augsburg of New York.”
- pp. 25–28: Agricola’s *De Re Metallica* (1556): the “detractors” passage, “there is greater detriment from mining than the value of the metals” (Brechin’s emphasis, p. 25). [HOOVER: the Hoovers’ 1912 translation is the standard English; check whether Brechin cites it → see later sections/notes.] Rio Tinto revived by Rothschild capital (p. 26). Railroad “came out of the mine” (p. 27).
- pp. 29–30: the “James Marshall myth.” Washington knew of California’s gold before the war. Larkin to Buchanan, May 4, 1846 (n9). Polk, Dec. 5, 1848 (n10). *California Star*: Yerba Buena “destined to be the Liverpool or New York of the Pacific.”
- **p. 31–32 (LAND):** placer bonanza brief. “The real fortunes were made by city-based financiers in hardrock mercury mining, by commission merchants, and, above all, by those speculating in land and engaging in fraud on an epic scale.” **“The monopolization of land by whatever means necessary laid the foundations for dynastic wealth. For those who’d gotten land cheap or free, there could never be too many immigrants to boost the value of the Golden State’s real estate.”** (n13). Gold peak $81M in 1852; $445M in 1848–58.
- p. 31: Society of California Pioneers (1850), membership restricted to pre-1850 arrivals: a self-made *ancien régime*.
- p. 33: capital channeled “from Eastern and European cities” into flumes [IVORY: Eastern capital]. Hydraulicking (Matteson, Connecticut, 1853). SF foundries supplied pipe: “Iron begot gold.”
- pp. 34–36: hydraulic networks. 5,726 mi of flumes and ditches within a decade; $30M in ditches by 1882. Irving M. Scott: boulders “jump twenty feet in the air,” while “the real hydraulic mine presents a wild and desolate appearance” (p. 36). Fig. 9 caption: these were “not enterprises of the independent miners … but rather required city-based banks and exchanges to raise capital.”
- pp. 36–38: SF Stock and Exchange Board formed Sept. 11, 1862, the first U.S. mining exchange. Brokers were the “Forty Thieves.” 1,300 mining companies within a year, “most of them fraudulent.” The Exchange channeled US and European capital. *Chronicle* 1876: SF wealth “levels the forests.”
- **pp. 38–43 (Ralston’s Ring):** Bank of California (1864). Ralston invited **Darius Ogden Mills** as “titular president” for “probity.” “All of the bank’s directors were heavily involved in mining.” By 1868 the Ring controlled the Exchange (p. 38–39). William Sharon was the Ring’s agent in Virginia City: he “devoured mines, mills, business associates, transportation, judges, legislators, and Sierra timberlands” (p. 39). Ralston also funded “political and judicial bribery.” **Huntington to Hopkins: “I think time will show that Ralston has got a larger institution than he is able to run”; Ralston’s code name for Huntington was “Hungry” (p. 39).** Ralston/Sharon/Mills built a vertical Comstock monopoly (mines, mills, railroad). Insider information let them manipulate the market (p. 41). Stevenson: the Exchange was “a great pump … pumping up the savings of the lower quarters into the pockets of the millionaires upon [Nob Hill]” (p. 41).
- p. 39: George Hearst’s sixth interest in the Ophir launched “a dynasty.”
- p. 43: Comstock output ~$350M over 25 years. Only five mines paid more in dividends than they collected in assessments. Alexander del Mar: the Lode “cost five times what it added to the economy” (n14). South-of-Market foundries had >1,000 men by 1861.
- pp. 44–48: Tahoe/Sierra deforestation. Grant H. Smith: 600M ft of lumber and 2M cords of firewood by 1880. “The graveyard of the Sierra forests.” Sharon acquired timberlands “for the Bank Crowd” as a way “to secretly siphon profits away from the mines” (p. 44).
- **p. 48: “in the infamous winter of 1862, Governor-elect Leland Stanford had to be rowed from his home to his inauguration.”** (Sacramento flood; verify; widely reported Jan. 10, 1862.) North Bloomfield (Ralston, 1866): >$1M, 47 mi of ditch, 8,000-ft tunnel.
- p. 48–49: the profit-takers wore “broadcloth and stovepipes”; “Gold and silver acted as fertilizer to San Francisco’s real estate values.”
- pp. 49–53: Bayard Taylor (*New York Tribune*) poems. Anti-Debris Society (1878) sued in federal court through “an absentee landowner living in New York” (Woodruff). **Sawyer decision Jan. 7, 1884** (permanent injunction against North Bloomfield). Brechin: it “merely confirmed the rights attached to one form of real estate over another” (p. 51). The industry moved abroad. SF dredges (Risdon, Union Iron Works).
- **pp. 53–58 (mining engineers; Hoover; IVORY):** Rickard: the profession is “the herald of empire.” Clubs: “Cosmos, Metropolitan, or Bohemian Clubs” (p. 53). **John Hays Hammond: “With the possible exception of fellow Californian Herbert Hoover, he was the most famous and highly paid engineer of his time”** (p. 53–54). Colleagues called his autobiography “full of lies” (n18, Spence 324, 336). Born SF 1855, moved to New York “to be near abundant capital.” Family friends with **D. O. Mills**, who took Bank of California/Comstock/Tahoe winnings to New York and built the Mills Building at Wall and Broad, “favored by mining companies and engineers” (p. 54). **n19: Mills’s daughter married Whitelaw Reid (*NY Tribune*). “Mills’s son, Ogden, married into the landed aristocracy of the Hudson River valley and his son became Herbert Hoover’s secretary of the treasury”** (Ogden L. Mills). The Mills and Hammond families financed Milham Exploration. **[IVORY ×3; HOOVER.]** Hamilton Smith (North Bloomfield) formed the London Exploration Co. with Rothschild backing (1886). “Californians were managing half the mines on the Rand.” Hammond worked for Barnato and Rhodes. His flogging quote (n23). He ran guns before the Boer War, was jailed in Pretoria, then went to Siberia and to Mexico “for the Mills and Guggenheim families.” Taft’s adviser, kept off the vice-presidential ticket by Guggenheim ties. Mansion in DC, estate at Gloucester, Mass. (p. 56). He lived in a “tight nexus of exclusive clubs, schools, resorts.” [Hammond = Yale Sheffield 1876; Brechin mentions only Freiberg, p. 55. Verify.]
- **p. 58: “By 1912, when Henry and Lou Hoover translated and republished his book …”** **ERROR in Brechin: the translator was Herbert C. Hoover (with Lou Henry Hoover), not “Henry.”** Cross-check Whyte (CANON-017, De Re Metallica). Log it as do-not-import.
- pp. 58–59: “Deferred costs.” DeGroot 1871 on “wrecks of stranded enterprise.” Valley critics said benefits drained to “capitalists based in San Francisco, London, Paris, and New York.” 1882 estimate: the public paid $2–5 for every $1 the mine owners pocketed.
- pp. 59–61: North Bloomfield reported >$2.8M profit, 1866–84. Mines were “close corporations” hiding inside information. G. K. Gilbert (USGS, 1905–09): 1.5 billion cubic yards of sediment, ~8× the Panama Canal excavation. The Corps of Engineers shifted the costs “from assessments on the mining companies, to the farmers, to the taxpayer” (p. 61). **State power absorbing private costs.**
- pp. 61–63: New Almaden (Santa Clara Valley) mercury. ~800,000 cu yd of roasted cinnabar dumped, “permanently poisoning the streams of the western Santa Clara Valley and San Francisco Bay” (p. 62). Comstock lost >7,000 tons of quicksilver. Oakland banned smelters (1870). **UC professor W. J. V. Osterhout told the *Chronicle* in 1906 that smelter fumes were not harmful.** The university as industry consultant (p. 63). The Guggenheims’ 1908 smelter plan was opposed by Hillsborough/Burlingame mining men.
- pp. 63–64: Olmsted (in California 1863–65): SF populated by “thriftless, fortune-hunting … vagabonds”; “there is no society.” Norris, *The Octopus*: ranchers “worked their ranches as … they had worked their mines”; “After us the deluge.”
- pp. 64–67: transfer technology. Hallidie (wire rope → cable car, Aug. 1, 1873, Nob Hill). “Those who financed the cable lines extended them into whatever dune field they had already … bought cheap” (p. 65). Real estate + transit. **Hallidie was a UC Regent; the Regents named the Hallidie Building (1917) for him** (p. 66).
- pp. 67–70: “Financial districts as inverted minescapes.” Deidesheimer square set → skyscraper. D. O. Mills turned mining profits into “vertical rental property on downtown lots” (p. 67). **Hearst to George Hearst, 1885: “The landlords are always a wealthy class. Every infant born in a country makes their land more valuable … every atom of humanity added to the struggling mass means another figure to his bank account”** (p. 68, n31). Land-value doctrine stated outright.

## Ch. 2 “Water Mains and Bloodlines” (pp. 71–117)
- Epigraph: *SF Chronicle* editorial, Aug. 6, 1989: “Economic growth … depends on population growth. Population growth depends absolutely on guaranteed … supplies of good quality drinking water.”
- p. 71: “Power veils itself … elite families marry to agglomerate wealth … they share a common interest in obtaining water to increase the value of their land.” Method: use engineers’ memoirs to trace power.
- pp. 72–73: Sunol Water Temple (Willis Polk, 1910) for William B. Bourn II, who bought Spring Valley in 1907 to sell it to the city “at a handsome profit.” Bourn inherited the Empire Mine and attended Cambridge University. [IVORY-adjacent: English elite education.]
- pp. 73–75: Roman aqueducts and Frontinus (context).
- **p. 76 (STATE POWER → LAND):** in 1858 George Ensign got **the power of eminent domain from the legislature** for private municipal water companies. It “gave the men who ran the pioneer water companies, like those who ran the railroads, the opportunity to acquire virtual empires of real estate.” Spring Valley was “nearly as hated by its own customers as the Southern Pacific.” **“California’s water companies served as the chief instruments for land speculators”** (n1).
- p. 77: 1856 consolidation shrank SF to 47 sq mi and created San Mateo County, “six times larger.” City leaders “wanted San Mateo back.” Plutocrats’ estates on old ranchos (the Howards).
- pp. 77–79: von Schmidt’s Pilarcitos diversion (water arrived July 4, 1862). Lake Tahoe & SF Water Works scheme (1865), mainly to supply hydraulic mines and raise land values along the route. Nevada resisted. William Stewart proposed a Tahoe national park (1900).
- pp. 79–80: Hermann Schussler, Spring Valley engineer for 50 years and consultant to capitalists. His villa sat between the Parrott and Howard estates. **Schussler “advised and insisted upon the timely acquisition of 100,000 acres of increasingly valuable watersheds, reservoir sites, water rights and rights of way”** (p. 80). Crystal Springs Dam sits on the San Andreas Fault.
- pp. 80–84: Ralston hired Olmsted. Olmsted proposed a drought-tolerant linear park (Van Ness valley) but also sold parks by real estate: Central Park adjacent property rose “1,000 percent in only five years” (p. 81). The park was meant to legalize squatter claims and “quiet contested land titles” (p. 81). The city hired William Hammond Hall (Golden Gate Park, 1871–76). Hall’s cousins were **John Hays Hammond** and Richard Pindell Hammond Jr. (surveyor general; president of the park commission) (p. 82). Hall in *Overland Monthly*, 1873: “Water is required for some large city, and forthwith an area many times as great is robbed of its rivulets and brooks—and its fertility” (p. 84, n4). [Cardinal cross-ref: Olmsted later laid out the Stanford campus, 1886–88. Not in Brechin.]
- pp. 85–86: Schussler’s Virginia City siphon (July 29, 1873). Flood: “Everything can be done nowadays; the only question is—will it pay?” Ralston’s Palace Hotel was meant to sell a land scheme south of Market. Ralston, Mills and Sharon built “an intricate financial web … in which money and assets could be moved about secretly, to their private enrichment” (p. 86). **Spring Valley “consistently bribed the very city supervisors required by law to supervise it.”** 1870: ~$480,000 distributed from $816,859 revenue = 59% (n5).
- pp. 86–89: Ralston bought Alameda Creek rights and Spring Valley, using bank certificates priced above the stock’s value. He sold his Alameda rights to the company for a $900,000 profit (n6). He planned to sell the company to the city for $5M profit. The Palace cost nearly 3× its estimate. The supervisors refused.
- **pp. 89–91 (Bank of California crash, Aug. 26, 1875):** Sharon dumped Comstocks, starting the run. Ralston owed the bank nearly $5M. Mills swore “we were all amazed and astonished and grieved.” Ralston drowned. Sharon: “Best thing he could have done” (n7). **Sharon’s “sack-bearers” had bribed his Senate election in Nevada** (n8). Sharon and Mills recast Ralston as a philanthropist. The widow got $50,000. Sharon took the Palace, Belmont, Burlingame Park and Spring Valley control. Flora Sharon married Lord Fermor-Hesketh; Frederick married a Tevis.
- **pp. 92–93 (FIELD):** the Sharon–Hill case. Judge Deady: “property and position are in themselves some certain guaranty of truth” (n13). **Field’s ruling came amid evident conflicts: “Sharon had, after all, loaned Field large sums of money and reserved a complimentary suite for him at the Palace Hotel … while defense counsel Newlands had endorsed his 1884 presidential bid.”** Hill shouted, “How much did Newlands pay you?” Terry was killed by Field’s bodyguard (1889). **Corroborates White (CANON-056): Field took favors from the railroad and banking elite.**
- **pp. 93–95 (Newlands; IVORY):** Newlands married Clara Sharon (1873) and became lead trustee of the Sharon estate. He lost the 1887 Senate bid to George Hearst and moved to Nevada in 1888: “I ought to identify myself politically as a means of influence in matters and litigation relating to the Estate” (p. 93–94). The estate’s Nevada holdings were “second only to those of the Central Pacific Railroad, with which he was closely connected.” He married the niece of **Ward McAllister** (New York society). **“the Yale-educated blue blood”** (p. 94). He developed **Chevy Chase** (1,720 acres, Maryland) from Dupont Circle lands bought with Stewart. Burlingame, “the Tuxedo Park of the West” (R. P. Hammond Jr.), then **Hillsborough (1910), “one of the nation’s premier power centers,” like “the men’s clubs of Nob Hill and the Bohemian Grove”** (p. 94).
- pp. 95–97: second generation “marrying European titles and one another.” **“Phantom capital”:** private trusts and estate companies form “an informal economy as invisible to the public as the black market,” run by family lawyers, chief among them Newlands (p. 96–97).
- pp. 97–98: Secrecy. N. W. Aldrich, *Old Money* (n18): the patriciate held “complete domination of all educational and cultural institutions … exceptionally favored treatment at the hands of political and civic officers … All these assets were hereditary.” Office “often proves highly lucrative in the service of phantom capital seeking to tap the public treasury for private gain” (p. 97). Will Tevis to his sister: “ours is the only one that has not been mulcted by the lawyers and politicians … no one of us has ever trusted himself with a statement of our business outside of our own office” (n19). The Tevis, Hearst, Haggin, Sharon, Crocker and Mills estates interlocked. **Newlands’s law partner William F. Herrin (Spring Valley attorney, then chief counsel of the Central/Southern Pacific) sat on the Sharon Estate board** (n20). Judge James M. Allen, another partner, was a director of the Sharon Estate and the Bank of California. In 1909 the estate used a cipher: Newlands was “Pine,” Herrin “Locust” (p. 98).
- pp. 98–99: Peninsula estates were land banks: founders likely bought them expecting subdivision. Spring Valley was “largely owned and run by the men of Hillsborough.” **Newlands in court: “This Company … occupies the position of a tax-gatherer … Has the populace … ever shown any fairness or moderation when arrayed against wealth?”** Brechin: “water utilities exist primarily to nourish real estate, not people” (n21). Progressive aristocrats knew the costs “could only be borne by the taxpayer” (p. 99).
- pp. 99–102: James D. Phelan inherited $11.5M (1892), including San Jose property (n22). He pushed “Greater San Francisco.” Hetch Hetchy (in Yosemite National Park since 1890) was listed as USGS Reservoir Site No. 33. **Phelan hired USGS coast representative J. B. Lippincott privately “to quietly file claims to the valley in Phelan’s name.”** Hitchcock to TR: the survey was “made surreptitiously” (p. 101). Phelan backed the 1901 right-of-way law (n24).
- **pp. 102–104 (Newlands self-dealing):** State engineer Hall moonlighted “confidentially” for capitalists, foremost Newlands on Sharon estate lands. Hall: “Irrigation was then the coming political fad.” Hall foresaw that “only the public treasury could bear such costs, but the payoff for those who owned the land would be correspondingly great” (p. 103). Reclamation Act (June 17, 1902). **Newlands Project: “Newlands had earlier instructed Hall to investigate the valley’s irrigation potential and had bought ranches there in the 1890s, which … he sold off to settlers and back to the government for reservoir sites” (n25).** Donner Lake land too. James Newlands Jr.: “we will realize $100,000” (n26, Sept. 1, 1903). A 1914 socialist paper accused Newlands and the SP; “nothing came of it” (n25). Reno: Newlands Heights (n27). **A clear case of legislation tied to the sponsor’s land positions.**
- **pp. 104–106 (Owens Valley; IVORY: Harriman):** Lippincott (Reclamation Service) gave Eaton and Mulholland land office records and withdrew public lands “ostensibly for a reclamation project.” **San Fernando syndicate: H. E. Huntington, “Southern Pacific president E.H. Harriman,” Otis and Chandler took options after the reconnaissance** (p. 105). The 1905 bond passed 14:1 amid rumors of reservoirs drained at night; $23M more in 1907. TR: city use is “a hundred or thousandfold more important” (p. 106).
- pp. 107–108: Will Tevis’s Bay Cities Water Co. asked $10.5M, “with a cool million reserved as payoff to city officials.” Gifts from PG&E, United Railroads and Parkside Realty to a corrupt city hall. 1906 earthquake. Schmitz administration removed for bribery (n28).
- pp. 108–110: Colby (Sierra Club, 1912) on press “willful suppression of facts.” The eastern press predicted $122M in contracts. The $45M bond passed Jan. 14, 1910. **Franklin K. Lane (Phelan’s friend; former SF city attorney) became Interior Secretary. Raker Act signed Dec. 19, 1913.** Richardson: “the workings of politics.” **“Yet Hetch Hetchy did precisely what it was designed to do—raise land values”** (p. 110).
- pp. 113–117: O’Shaughnessy (SP, Burlingame plan). Seven bonds ≈ $102M plus $41M for Spring Valley (1930) (n31). Backers “deliberately kept the initial estimate down to $45 million” (n32). Bondholders included W. B. Bourn. Route chosen to stimulate growth “through the southern Bay Area and up the peninsula” (p. 115). [Cardinal: the Peninsula/Santa Clara growth corridor feeds Stanford-area land value; Brechin does not name Stanford here.] Pulgas Water Temple near Filoli (Oct. 28, 1934, Ickes).
- Off-topic within ch2: Roman aqueducts (pp. 73–75, 80) and Muir biography (p. 102). Context, read in full.

## Ch. 3 “The Scott Brothers: Arms and the Overland Monthly” (pp. 121–169)
- Read in full; Cardinal relevance is moderate. Militarism and empire as a state-money pipeline; one Stanford trustee.
- pp. 121–125: Phelan’s California Volunteers’ Monument (Aug. 12, 1906). Frémont. Army officers “felt entitled to a fair share of those lands and to the profits from the cities they hoped would grow upon them” (p. 123). Stevenson’s regiment were “military colonists in the tradition of the Roman Legion.” **Frisbie used his influence to move the naval yard inland to Mare Island; Vallejo deeded him the adjacent town land Dec. 9, 1854 (n2). Base location as land speculation.** Gwin’s 1852 Senate report: “defensive or offensive” (n1).
- pp. 125–127: Donahue’s Union Iron Works. Peter Donahue laid the SF–San Jose railroad during the Civil War; Irving M. Scott built its first heavy locomotive (p. 126). Scott claimed ~90% of Comstock machinery.
- **pp. 127–128:** Scott moved the UIW to Potrero (1883), “almost certainly” anticipating Sawyer’s 1884 injunction. Charleston contract (1886, n7). Scott to his men: arms were “the key to unlock the Treasury” (1887, n8).
- **p. 128 (STANFORD; IVORY):** Hammond consulted for Scott. **Hammond’s “college classmate, Walker Blaine,” introduced Scott to Secretary of State James G. Blaine, helping win the $3M battleship *Oregon*; Hammond admitted UIW’s “bid was not the lowest one received.”** [IVORY: Hammond and Walker Blaine were both Yale class of 1876. Verify.] **Irving M. Scott was a director of the Central Pacific Railroad and “served as a trustee of Stanford University and regent of the University of California”** (p. 128). Check against the canon list of original Stanford trustees.
- p. 130: Henry T. Scott directed the Crocker Estate Company, was mayor of Burlingame (Newlands’s friend), and later headed Pacific Telephone & Telegraph. Irving Scott funded the *Overland Monthly* (motto “Devoted to the Development of the Country”). The *NY Sun* said he used it for Republican advancement (n12).
- pp. 130–132: *Overland* editorials: Aryan westward migration, a Nicaragua canal, transcontinental subsidies to let “the military power of the central government” reach territory (n18), and “friendly dismemberment” of China (1898, n20).
- pp. 132–137: Spanish-American War. The Navy had planned the seizure of the Philippines two years earlier (n21). Newlands’s “ripe fruit” speech (n23). **Anti-imperialists included “Stanford president David Starr Jordan”** (p. 135). Scott: “Might makes right”; critics “as traitorous … as Benedict Arnold” (n30). Shafter and “Howlin’ Jake” Smith flanked Phelan in 1906. *Overland* editorial, 1900: “The Subjugation of Inferior Races” (n33).
- pp. 138–142: Philippine concessions. Vanderbilt-led New York syndicate railroads [IVORY: Eastern financiers]. Fleishacker and Spreckels sugar; Poniatowski (W. H. Crocker’s brother-in-law) got a Palawan concession. Judge Belcher: SF growth requires occupation; real estate stimulus (n37).
- pp. 142–144: Scott sold the cruiser *Chitose* to Japan (launched Jan. 22, 1898) and pitched battleships to the czar.
- pp. 144–154: City Beautiful. Burnham Plan (1904–06), hosted by Phelan at the St. Francis (a Crocker Estate hotel, Henry Scott head). Phelan and Newlands: “only centralized authority and public financing could achieve proper order, and that this order would ultimately redound to the benefit of that class that owned land” (p. 151). McKinley stayed with Henry T. Scott (1901). Irving Scott was floated for VP at the 1900 Republican convention (p. 149). Elihu Root: “marked humanity” (n48).
- pp. 154–169: race and Yellow Peril. 1906 school segregation; Japan’s war with Russia financed by “New York and London banking houses” (n59) [IVORY-adjacent]. Hammond called for an Anglo alliance against Japan (n70). Mackay cable to Manila. Great White Fleet (May 6, 1908). 1913 Alien Land Law: the legislature “prohibited Japanese from owning land” (n88). **State power over land along racial lines.** FDR (Asst. - **Mills family:** D. O. Mills was a former UC regent and Whitelaw Reid’s father-in-law (n28). Ogden L. Mills, D. O.’s grandson, was Hoover’s Treasury Secretary 1932–33. Hearst Oil was in the Mills Buildings and partnered with the Mills family (ch6 n20). Cerro de Pasco was backed by Morgan, Vanderbilt, Frick and Mills (ch5).
- **Eastern finance into the West:** Morgan, Vanderbilts, Frick, Guggenheims, Rothschilds (Anaconda), Keene. PG&E formed in 1905 “with the backing of Wall Street capital” (p. 264). Schwab/Bethlehem bought Union Iron Works. Kuhn, Loeb sold Japan’s war bonds (ch3 n59). A Vanderbilt-led NY syndicate built Philippine railroads (p. 140).
- **Hearst:** educated at St. Paul’s and Harvard; Wall Street used the NY *Herald* against him in 1906 (ch5 n37).
- **Bohemian Grove** as the East–West switchboard: Hoover, Warren, McLaughlin and the Sept. 1942 Manhattan Project meeting (p. 318).
- **E. H. Harriman** appears twice: in the San Fernando/Owens Valley syndicate with Huntington and Otis (p. 105), and in the graft probe, where “even E.H. Harriman, head of the Southern Pacific system, were named” (ch4). *Not in Brechin:* Dulles, Skull and Bones by name, and Delano, which appears only as FDR’s middle name (verified by full-text search).
