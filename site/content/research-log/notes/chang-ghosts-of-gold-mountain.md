## Ch. 2 “Gold Mountain” (pp. 38–54): read in full
- Cardinal relevance: low to moderate. Legal exclusion, the “free labor” vs. coerced-labor dispute (this bears on how the CPRR got its labor), and Six Companies.
- p. 39 ¶4: the Nationality Act of 1790 limited naturalization to “free, white persons”; the 1866 Act and Fourteenth Amendment left Chinese out.
- p. 39–40 ¶6: PMSC’s *Great Republic* (1867–79) carried ~10,000 Chinese. In Nov. 1869 it brought ~1,300, “many likely coming for railroad work.”
- p. 43 ¶13: about 500 of 58,000 gold seekers in 1850 were Chinese. From 1852 to the early 1860s, 6,000–7,000 arrived a year.
- p. 44 ¶14: *Alta*, 1854: Chinese would do the work white men would “never” do. “Roads have to be made, and railroads will soon follow.”
- pp. 44–45 ¶15–16: the 1876 federal commission could not agree on numbers (67,000 to 210,000+).
- **p. 45 ¶18 (CPRR labor):** the *NY Evening Post* (1869): “nearly the whole force which built the Central Pacific railroad were brought over as free laborers.” They came under consul-approved contracts arranged by an unidentified American businessman working with Chinese merchants: passage was advanced, a deposit covered return of remains, and wages paid off the advance (some mortgaged real property). [IVORY-adjacent: an East Coast paper as character witness.]
- pp. 46–48 ¶20–25: Six Companies (huiguan). Leung Cook testified to the 1876 state commission (Ning Yeung: $5 exit fee; repatriation of remains).
- pp. 48–52 ¶27–32: photographers Carleton Watkins and Lai Yong. Lai Yong’s 1873 “The Chinese Question from a Chinese Standpoint”: Chinese had “toiled patiently to build your railroads.”
- **pp. 52–54 ¶34–37 (coercion counter-evidence; CPRR):** attorney Daniel Cleveland’s unpublished manuscript (late 1868 or early 1869; his report reached Seward). Credit-ticket arrivals “are taken possession of by their white owners, and are treated like slaves, watched and guarded, until they reach the end of their journey, and are delivered to the Railroad Company.” Men with bludgeons guarded them on the tug. They were shipped upriver to Sacramento and **locked in railroad boxcars**; some jumped and died. **This conflicts with Chang’s own “came freely” framing (¶18–19) and with the CPRR’s later testimony. Chang presents both without reconciling them.** It is a key item for Cardinal on CPRR labor procurement.

## Ch. 3 “Central Pacific” (pp. 55–74): read in full. CORE (Stanford, the Big Four, labor procurement, federal subsidy)
- p. 55 ¶2: by summer 1868, 10,000 Railroad Chinese made up 90% of the force.
- **p. 56 ¶4 (STANFORD; IVORY):** Asa Whitney (NY merchant, China trade) promoted the Pacific road: “Through us must be the route to Asia.” **“Among those who shared the vision was a young Leland Stanford in upstate New York, who listened as Whitney sought to persuade Leland’s father to join his crusade”** (n. to p. 56: “Biographical Sketch,” *Memorial Addresses on … Leland Stanford*, Senate, 53rd Cong., 2nd sess., 1894, 11; a hagiographic source).
- pp. 56–57 ¶5–6: Sen. Benton imagined Chinese labor and intermarriage: “Commerce is a great civilizer.” Speer’s *The Oriental* (1855) predicted “Chinese builders of iron roads.”
- p. 57–58 ¶8 [IVORY]: the “New York−based Panama Railway Company” brought 1,000 Chinese to Panama in 1854, and 50% died.
- p. 58 ¶9: in 1858 the California Central hired 50 Chinese, and Chinese worked on the San Jose–San Francisco line.
- p. 58–59 ¶10–13: Zhigang of the Burlingame delegation [IVORY: Anson Burlingame, Massachusetts/Harvard] rode a train on Apr. 16, 1868.
- p. 59–61 ¶14–35: Gov. McDougal praised the Chinese (1852); Gov. Bigler sought a Foreign Miners’ Tax and head tax (1852) and later sympathized with the Confederacy. **n. to p. 61: Bigler was “later in his career … appointed a federal commissioner to oversee the construction of the Pacific railroad”** (state-power crossover). Norman Asing: “aristocracy of skin.”
- **p. 61 ¶37 (STANFORD; key quote):** in his 1862 gubernatorial (inaugural) address, Stanford said: “The settlement among us of an inferior race is to be discouraged”; “Asia, with her numberless millions, sends to our shores the dregs of her population”; the Chinese are “a degraded and distinct people” exercising “a deleterious influence upon the superior race.” It drew warm applause and remains “his best-known comments on the Chinese.” (n. to p. 62: governors.library.ca.gov; Chang’s own essay “The Chinese and the Stanfords,” in Chang & Fishkin eds., *Chinese and the Iron Road*, Stanford UP 2019; Julie Cain M.A. thesis, 2011.)
- **pp. 61–63 ¶38–40 (STANFORD; Chang’s defense):** his sentiments were “apparently politically motivated.” In private the Stanfords kept Chinese servants: cook Moy Jin Kee and his brother Moy Jin Mun, whom Jane reportedly proposed to adopt and gave an engraved gold ring (family history). In 1862 Chinese herbalist Yee Fung Cheung treated Jane’s pulmonary illness. **“Stanford himself, as the president of the CPRR, … would be responsible for attracting more Chinese to America than any other single individual.”** He “directly employed hundreds of Chinese on his many private and commercial properties … including at his stock farm in Palo Alto.” “At the end of his life, Stanford rued his earlier prejudice.” **They “had, of course, helped him become one of the richest men in the world.”** All of ¶38–40 is sourced “Ibid.” to Chang’s own essay and the Cain thesis. **Caution: this is a Stanford-affiliated author’s mitigation, and the “rued” claim needs its primary (Stanford’s 1887 Pacific Railway Commission testimony?).**
- **p. 63–64 ¶42 (federal subsidy):** Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act (July 1862): land grants, bonds and loans; competition “encouraged financial malfeasance, slipshod work, political intrigue, bribery, and industrial sabotage.” **“Eventually, the two railroad companies obtained more than 175 million square miles of public land in subsidies”: an ERROR (impossible; the whole US is ~3.8M sq mi).** Crédit Mobilier. (n. cites Bain and White, *Railroaded*.)
- p. 64 ¶44: the *Sacramento Union* (Oct. 14, 1865) said the line needed 50,000 tons of iron and 1.1M ties. The CPRR started at “Colfax, fifty-five miles north of Sacramento, where the line actually began” (odd phrasing; the line began at Sacramento).
- **p. 66 ¶48 (STANFORD; dates):** Stanford broke ground in Jan. 1863. **“Assuming office in 1861, Stanford had lobbied vigorously for federal government support.”** [Check: he was elected Sept. 1861 and inaugurated Jan. 10, 1862.] The Big Four established the CPRR in June 1861 “anticipating the federal government’s decision.” They were thought “a little off,” even “insane” (n. to p. 67: Alfred E. Davis testimony, PRC vol. 6, 3620–26).
- p. 67 ¶49–50: the leaders “because of their racial prejudice … initially wanted only whites.” Clement: “It was impossible to get white labor.” From Jan. 1863 to June 1864 the line reached only Newcastle (31 mi). They considered Confederate prisoners, freedmen and Mexicans (n.: Saxton; Oscar Lewis, *Big Four*).
- p. 67–68 ¶51–52: Crocker’s manservant Ah Ling may have suggested Chinese labor (conjecture by Crocker descendants; Chang’s 2016 conversation). Strobridge: “I will not boss Chinese.” Crocker: “Did they not build the Chinese wall …?” (PRC vol. 7, 3660.)
- pp. 68–72 ¶53–66: Hung Wah, Auburn labor contractor (b. 1831/35, d. 1931). In 1862 he contracted 150 workers for 60% of the gold dust (*Roush v. Terry*). Aug. 1863 ad: “I will furnish any number of Chinese laborers to work on Rail Roads.” *People v. Hall* (1854) barred Chinese testimony. Cleveland to J. Ross Browne, July 21, 1868 (forwarded to Seward).
- **p. 72 ¶67 (CPRR):** the Jan. 1864 CPRR payroll is the first firm evidence of Chinese workers: Hung Wah signed for ~$675. Crocker: “50 Chinamen” then “50 more.” They were put to work “on what was known as the **Dutch Flat–Donner Lake Wagon Road**, which the CPRR would use for access.” [Cardinal: the wagon road was the Big Four’s privately owned toll road (the “Dutch Flat swindle”). Chang omits that ownership; cross-check with White.]
- **p. 73 ¶68–69 (wages):** by spring 1865, 2,000–3,000 Chinese and 800 whites. Chinese were paid ~$26/month and paid their own room and board; whites got “perhaps 30 percent more” (n.: *Sacramento Union*, May 1, 1865; Crocker PRC 7:3659–60).

It took little for contracting but “required that it control provisioning.” Profits came from tools, clothes, liquor, opium and food. **“The arrangement was lucrative, especially for the Crocker family, which benefited at each level of the operation. Charles Crocker, a director of the CPRR, also headed its main construction company, Crocker Construction. As a boss of one company, he therefore essentially paid himself as the head of the other.”** **Clark W. Crocker** was a chief partner of Sisson, Wallace (n. to p. 114: renamed Sisson, Crocker & Co.; *Wallace v. Sisson*, June 9, 1893, *California Unreported Cases* vol. 4, 34–47). This is the strongest money item in the chapter; cross-ref White CANON-059.
- p. 113–115 ¶39–41: contractors made their money on provisioning rather than wage cuts. Ah Louis’s records are at Stanford’s Green Library.

## Ch. 6 “The Summit” (pp. 121–137): read by keyword-plus-lead scan, with relevant paragraphs in full
- Lower-value material, scanned: tunnel engineering, Hart photo analysis and snowsheds (pp. 125–137).
- p. 121–122 ¶2–5: on July 4, 1866, Chinese workers rode in the CPRR parade in Sacramento. Crocker: “the iron horse was now puffing his nostrils at an altitude of over 3,600 feet.” Six Companies: about a quarter of California’s 58,000 Chinese worked for the CPRR or on “other public improvements.”
- p. 122–123 ¶6: the line reached Dutch Flat (mile 67) and then Cisco (mile 92, Nov. 1866). The company ran 100 locomotives.
- **pp. 123–124 ¶8–9 (STANFORD):** in his 1866 report, Stanford called the work “the most difficult ever yet surmounted by any railroad in the United States, if not in Europe … a herculean task.” He said it was costly because the company had to pay in gold for “all labor.” It wanted 15,000 workers but got 10,000, kept 6,000 through winter, and planned 20,000. The California Powder Works near Santa Cruz (opened 1864) had 275 workers, all Chinese.
- p. 125 ¶11: a five-mile single tunnel was considered (~$1M a mile).
- **p. 127 ¶17 (IVORY: New York):** in the winter of 1866–67 there were 44 storms. E. B. Crocker reminded “an impatient Collis Huntington in New York” that the summit “is a rough place in a storm.”
- **p. 136 ¶32:** nitroglycerine was made on site near Donner Lake, and an April 1866 explosion killed six workers. The company dropped it “not because of its danger but because of patent restrictions … Alfred Nobel, and government restrictions.”

## Ch. 7 “The Strike” (pp. 138–165): read in full, paragraph by paragraph
The main sources are the Crocker, Hopkins and Huntington letters (Huntington Papers microfilm), plus 1876 Senate testimony and 1887 PRC testimony.
- **p. 138 ¶2 (IVORY: New York capital):** in Jan. 1867, E. B. Crocker wrote to Huntington, “ensconced in New York City raising capital for the railroad project”: shrinking the force “can’t be done … it will be fatal.” (n.: E. B. Crocker to Huntington, Jan. 10 and 31, 1867.)
- **p. 139–140 ¶4 (STANFORD):** Stanford, E. H. Miller Jr. and S. S. Montague reported to the board on Jan. 5, 1867, asking for a “large force, probably twenty thousand” (PRC vol. 5, 3050–51). The company had 5,000 Chinese-language handbills printed to circulate “all over this state & in China.” No copy survives.
- **p. 140 ¶5 (MONEY / lifestyle):** “The top officers of the company assumed high financial risk and eventually became enormously wealthy.” Huntington lived at New York’s Metropolitan Hotel and then a Park Avenue mansion. **Stanford “had his own mansion in Sacramento, which he acquired when he was governor.”** Strobridge’s camp train housed about 500 mostly white workers.
- **p. 141–142 ¶8:** Chinese tunnelers outperformed Cornish miners (Crocker’s Senate testimony, Nov. 14, 1876, pp. 666–88).
- **p. 142 ¶9:** the Nov. 1866 payroll for Summit camp 51 “A” lists Chinese waiters at $0.66 a day, Chinese construction workers at $1 a day and white blacksmiths at $2.50 a day with no board deducted. (CSRM MS 79.)
- p. 143 ¶10: Clement on Chinese workers: “never took any particular interest in them, never cared about them so long as we got the work done” (PRC 6:3217).
- p. 143–144 ¶12–13: wages. White carpenters got $4 a day with board. Clement: white woodcutters $40 a month with board, Chinese $30 without. Gilliss: Chinese at the Summit Tunnel $31 a month without board; white foremen $120 with board.
- **p. 144–145 ¶14–16 (MONEY: admission under oath):** in Senate testimony, Crocker said Chinese were paid $35 a month and spent about 40¢ a day at the company store. Strobridge: “sometimes we did not pay for two months and perhaps more”; Chinese labor was “much cheaper. Their board is an important consideration.” Asked, **“Did you make more money out of [employing Chinese] than if you had employed white men?” Crocker: “I think I did.”** (*Report of the Joint Special Committee to Investigate Chinese Immigration*, 44th Cong. 2d sess., 1877, 671–88, 723–28.)
- p. 147 ¶22: Chinese insisted on pay in gold coin, unlike UP workers, who were paid in paper. Where the money went is unknown (“ghost money”).
- **p. 147–148 ¶23–26:** the strike of June 1867 was “the largest, or certainly close to it, workers’ strike against a private employer … to that date.” Chang argues that “it was the company’s vulnerability, not desperation among the Chinese,” that drove it. “The company was in shaky financial condition.”
- p. 149 ¶29: on May 16, 1867, E. B. Crocker wrote that “Several of our largest Capitalists” had toured the line. **“The cheapness is due in a great measure to the low price of Chinese labor.”**
- p. 149 ¶30: other employers were now hiring Chinese: “Our use of them has led hundreds of others to employ them.”
- **p. 150 ¶31 (STANFORD; Sisson link):** Stanford to Hopkins, May 18, 1867: Stanford had learned from a friend and from his “own personal Chinese contacts” of widespread unhappiness among Chinese workers contracted under “Sisson.” (Ties to ch. 5 ¶40 on Crocker family self-dealing.)
- p. 150 ¶32: the company raised Chinese wages from $31 to $35 a month in May 1867. Hopkins: work was “impossible” without the Chinese.
- **p. 151 ¶33 (STATE POWER):** “Desperate, the company appealed to the federal government to send ‘5,000 negroes to work building the road.’” (n.: E. B. Crocker to Huntington, June 4, 1867; Hopkins to Huntington, June 26, 1867.) **Needs verification:** which federal body (the Freedmen’s Bureau?) and whether there is a primary document. Chang’s note cites only private letters.
- p. 151 ¶34: a tunnel explosion near Cisco on June 19 killed a white man named Burns and “five Chinamen.”
- **pp. 151–152 ¶35–39:** on June 24, 1867, 3,000 Chinese struck from Cisco to Truckee. Demands: $40 a month (parity), a 10-hour day (some reports said 8), shorter tunnel shifts. The *Sacramento Daily Union* reported a protest against overseers’ “right … to either whip them or restrain them from leaving the road.” Chang notes this is uncorroborated.
- **p. 153–154 ¶40–42:** Strobridge’s wire: “chinamen have all struck for $40 & time to be reduced from 11 to 10 hours.” Hopkins: if they succeed, “they control.” E. B. Crocker, June 27: “they are getting smart … we would always be in their power. The only safe way for us is to inundate this state and Nevada with laborers, Freedmen, Chinese, Japanese.” Charles Crocker: “John Chinaman no make laws for me; I make laws for Chinaman.” **Hopkins: “A Negro labor force will tend to keep the chinese steady, as the chinese have kept the Irishmen quiet.”** He asked Huntington to send workers “from the East.” [IVORY]
- **p. 155–156 ¶44 (STATE POWER / coercion):** the strike was ended by cutting off provisions. E. B. Crocker credited the Chinese agents; Charles Crocker claimed he cut them off himself. “None of us went near them for a week … We did not want to exhibit anxiety.” Strikers were told to return or forfeit all June pay. Crocker’s security force would “shoot down any man”; **“the local sheriff and armed guards”** were on hand. (Cardinal: county police power deployed for the company.)
- p. 156 ¶45: Hopkins blamed “chinese gamblers and opium traders.” Charles Crocker suspected UP agents.
- **p. 156–157 ¶46:** “The CPRR, highly dependent on public funding and private investments,” needed to appear in control for investors.
- p. 157 ¶47: after the strike, pay quietly rose above $35 for skilled Chinese workers, from $26 in 1864.
- **p. 158 ¶51 (STATE POWER):** E. B. Crocker showed visiting Washington officials the work. A federal inspector, “a Mr. Johnson,” was “highly pleased,” and “his report to the Secretary of the Interior will be all that we can ask.” (n.: E. B. Crocker to Huntington, July 23, 1867.) Cardinal: the company’s confidence about the government inspector’s report.
- **p. 159 ¶53 (SMOKING-GUN class quote):** E. B. Crocker, Sept. 12, 1867: **“We want 100,000 chinamen here so as to bring the price of labor down.”** Huntington, Oct. 3, 1867: **“I like the idea of your getting over more Chinamen; it would be all the better for us and the state if then should a half million come over in 1868.”** The company worked with steamship companies on “favorable arrangements.” California’s 1860 population was under 400,000.
- p. 159–160 ¶54: later strikes. Stockton, 1869. The Santa Cruz Mountains (1870s), where a thousand Chinese built rail and timber lines. Tehachapi, 1875, 3,000 strikers.
- p. 161 ¶57: E. B. Crocker to Huntington, Aug. 10, 1867, describing seam blasts.
- p. 162 ¶59–61: snowsheds: 37 miles and 65 million feet of timber. Arthur Brown called the expense “appalling and unprecedented” (Brown to Stanford, July 25, 1887, PRC 5:2581).
- p. 163–164 ¶64: Gilliss: a slide at Tunnel No. 10 killed “fifteen or twenty Railroad Chinese.”
- p. 164 ¶66: Hopkins said Chinese at the summit went “blind” from snow glare. E. B. Crocker: “we have had to buy up all the cheap goggles in the market.”

## Ch. 10 “Beyond Promontory” (pp. 209–236): read in full, paragraph by paragraph
- p. 210 ¶2: “California-grown strawberries could go east, as they did with Stanford for his banquet at Promontory.”
- p. 210 ¶4 [IVORY]: the article “The Chinaman as a Railroad Builder” ran in 20+ papers, including the *National Intelligencer* and the New York–based *Scientific American*.

The Northern Pacific was the largest employer (6,000+). [IVORY: the NP was Jay Cooke-financed, though Chang doesn’t name him]
- p. 215 ¶17: immigration from 1869 to 1877 averaged 15,000 a year (20,000+ in 1873), “far lower than the grand numbers that were tossed around by the Big Four.”
- p. 215 ¶18 [IVORY]: New York had 300 Chinese laundries by 1879.
- p. 217 ¶21 [IVORY]: the New York *Merchants’ Magazine and Commercial Review* (Aug. 1869) on the “Coming Chinese.”
- p. 218 ¶22 [IVORY]: *Frank Leslie’s* series “The Coming Man” (Thomas W. Knox, 1870).
- p. 218 ¶23: Charles Crocker: “are men, good and true men.” Chang: “sincere respect for those who had made him very wealthy.”
- **p. 219 ¶24 (STATE POWER; IVORY):** the Burlingame Treaty (1868). Anson Burlingame was Lincoln’s minister to China and then China’s envoy. The treaty guaranteed free immigration. Cardinal: the treaty secured the CPRR’s labor pipeline.
- p. 219–220 ¶26: the 1870 Naturalization Act extended naturalization to people of African descent, but not Chinese. Chinese were barred until 1943.
- p. 220 ¶27: in 1868, SF Chinese leaders set aside $10,000 to repatriate about 300 bodies.
- **p. 221 ¶29 [IVORY]:** “Famed New York jurist and future U.S. attorney general **Edwards Pierrepont**” said at the **New York banquet for Burlingame’s Chinese embassy, June 23, 1868**, that shippers “take out dead Chinamen” and “bring back live ones.” (Pierrepont: Yale 1837. Web-verify if used.)
- p. 222 ¶32: in spring 1868, a gang identified as KKK lynched four Chinese at Grass Valley.
- **p. 222 ¶34:** “the company kept no records of personnel killed on the job.” The Lake Tahoe drowning legend (800 men) has “no evidence.”
- **pp. 222–223 ¶35:** Clement: “many laborers lost their lives.” Strobridge: men buried until spring (these quotes come from **1887 letters to Stanford** for the PRC; see ch. 5). Gilliss: 15–20 killed at Tunnel 10. Partridge: “a good many were frozen to death.”
- p. 223–224 ¶36: the Canadian Pacific analog: 600 to 3,000 deaths.
- p. 224–225 ¶40–53: a list of reported deaths, 1866–69. Colfax explosion (Apr. 1866); snowslides (Dec. 1866, Mar. 1867, 19+ killed); NY Evening Post, Dec. 1867 (22 killed); Truckee cabin (Jan. 1868, 5); and others.
- **p. 226–227 ¶54 (COMPANY PRIORITIES):** “The almost complete absence of attention to worker fatalities in the correspondence among the CPRR’s top leaders is itself evidence of the low regard.” Clement: “I never counted … never cared about them so long as we got the work done.” **E. B. Crocker to Huntington, Jan. 31, 1867: accidents would come “in spite of all precautions,” but the company must be ready so they “will not stop the tracklaying beyond.”**
- p. 227 ¶55: “the CPRR never issued any public statement acknowledging the death of any railroad worker.”
- **p. 227–228 ¶56–58 (death toll):** possibly 1,200 or more died, based on the June 1870 *Sacramento Reporter*: “accumulated bones of perhaps 1,200 Chinamen … about 20,000 pounds.” The n. concedes a same-day report of about 50 bodies, and that the figure is questioned. **Chang’s own bottom line (p. 230 ¶62): “likely well into the hundreds, if not substantially higher.”** Use that hedged line, not “1,200.”
- p. 229–230 ¶61–62: association records, 1850s–1869: 7.5% died in America. *Railroad Record*: 12%.
- p. 231 ¶65–67: LA massacre, 1871 (18 lynched). Truckee “Trout Creek Outrage” (June 1876, Caucasian League) and the “Truckee method”. Denver, 1880.

Do not import. Arthur’s veto quote, that Chinese “were largely instrumental in constructing the railways,” is usable once verified.
- p. 232 ¶70: Rock Springs, 1885: 28+ killed, and the victims were **Union Pacific** coal miners. [IVORY: UP/Eastern-owned]
- p. 233 ¶72: about 170 expulsion episodes. The Chinese population fell from 133,000 (1882) to 90,000 (1900).
- p. 233 ¶74–75: Sing Lum oral history: graves around **Delano**, Kern County, on the SP (Delano the town; not a Delano-family crossover, though the town was named for Columbus Delano, Grant’s Interior Secretary [IVORY-adjacent; verify]).

Cardinal: a Peninsula/Stanford-region railroad. Also note Alfred E. Davis (quoted p. 164) was Fair’s partner.
